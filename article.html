Fool the AI

A playable introduction to adversarial examples, online learning, and the robustness–accuracy tradeoff.
D-studyLab · 2026 · All demos run entirely in your browser — nothing is uploaded. Loading models…

You are about to meet a neural network that reads handwritten digits with 98.6% accuracy. Your job is to break it — with nothing but your own handwriting. And when you succeed, it will learn from your attack, right in front of you.

1 · A digit you can read, and it cannot

Neural networks for digit recognition are a solved problem — on average. The small convolutional network (CNN) below scores 98.6% on the standard MNIST test set. But averages hide individuals. Here are real test digits, written by real people, that a larger "referee" network reads correctly while our small model gets them wrong:

Demo 1 — Natural adversarial examples from MNIST

15 samples, all from the untouched MNIST test set — no pixel tricks, just handwriting that lives near the model's decision boundary.

Classic adversarial-example research perturbs pixels with gradient noise invisible to humans [2]. This article explores the other direction: adversarial examples that a human produces naturally, with a pen — shapes that sit inside the gap between how you read and how a small model reads.

Two properties matter for what comes next. First, the model is small on purpose (≈52k parameters): small enough that its feature maps are legible, and small enough to leave gaps a human can exploit. Second, everything you see — convolution, pooling, dense layers, softmax — is implemented in plain JavaScript and verified against PyTorch to a numerical error below 10-6, so the visualization is the computation, not a cartoon of it.

3 · Turning attack into a game: the referee

"Draw something the model misreads" is trivially easy — scribble garbage and every classifier fails. The interesting question is drawing something that humans still read correctly while the model fails. But putting a human judge in the loop makes the win condition subjective.

Our solution: a second, much larger CNN (≈200k parameters, 99.0% accuracy) acts as the referee. You are given a target digit. You win only if the referee reads your drawing as the target and the small adversary misreads it. The referee is strict enough to be a stand-in for "a human can read this" — it accepts 98.9% of ordinary MNIST handwriting — which makes the win condition objective, automatic, and honest.

Design note: the referee also solves the labeling problem for free. Because a win requires the referee to read the drawing as the target, every successful attack arrives with a trustworthy label attached — the game rule is a supervised-learning pipeline.

4 · Watch it learn

Here is the part we have not seen in other interactive ML explainers: when the model is fooled, it retrains — live, in your browser tab. We freeze the convolutional layers and run stochastic gradient descent on the dense head (≈52k weights), mixing your adversarial drawing with a replay buffer of 300 clean digits so the model doesn't forget what it already knows. The loss curve below is not an animation; it is the actual loss of the actual optimizer, step by step. (The JavaScript training loop is gradient-parity-verified against PyTorch's autograd to ~10-9.)

After training, the same drawing is re-classified — and the trick that worked seconds ago no longer works. In play-testing, all 15 natural adversarial examples from Demo 1 were "cured" by a single training round of at most 40 steps (~50 ms of compute; we slow it down so you can watch).

5 · The price of robustness

Training on your attacks is not free. Watch the "report card" below: the model's accuracy on 200 held-out ordinary digits, measured before and after each training round on this page.

Demo 5 — Report card (this page's session)

clean accuracy now – at page load – training rounds 0
Adversarial drawings live near decision boundaries, so teaching the model an ambiguous "0-that-looks-like-5" legitimately drags nearby real 5s across the boundary.

This is the robustness–accuracy tradeoff [3], usually a plot in a paper — here, a number you personally caused to drop. It also opens a strategy that players discover on their own: feed the model deliberately ambiguous shapes to carve new holes elsewhere. That strategy has a name in the security literature — data poisoning — and to our knowledge this is the first place you can commit it with a pen, for fun.

6 · Play, and what this is for

The full game — levels, a boss that persists and grows between sessions, a collection book of your successful deceptions, sound, and a scoring system that rewards fooling an increasingly educated opponent — is here:

▶ Play "Fool the AI" (Japanese UI; the pictures speak for themselves)

Beyond fun, the loop "humans attack → model retrains → humans must invent new attacks" is exactly dynamic adversarial data collection [4], and games that produce useful data as a side effect have a long lineage [5]. What we add is immediacy: the retraining is not a nightly server job but a spectacle the attacker watches — and we suspect that watching a model learn from you is a stickier explanation of "training" than any diagram.

Limitations. MNIST is a small, easy domain; the adversary's head is deliberately under-parameterized; and our referee is itself a CNN, so referee-and-adversary share some blind spots — a human-subjects study of where they disagree is future work, as is aggregating (opt-in, anonymous) attack drawings across players into seasonal boss updates.
